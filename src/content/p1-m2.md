# Privacy Architectures

In govtech AI, the most important question is rarely "Can this model do the task?" It is almost always: **"Where is the data?"** This module builds a practical mental model of how data moves through different AI architectures — and why that movement determines what is and isn't permissible at Nava.

> **Core principle:** The location of data processing determines legal exposure. Architectural decisions are privacy decisions.

---

## Two Architectures, Two Risk Profiles

### Cloud AI Architecture

In a typical cloud AI interaction, the flow looks like this:

```
Your device  →  HTTPS request  →  Vendor API server  →  Model inference  →  Response
               (data in transit)   (data at rest, vendor infra)
```

When you type a prompt into ChatGPT, Claude.ai, or Gemini, that text leaves your machine and travels to servers operated by OpenAI, Anthropic, or Google respectively. The vendor processes it, runs inference, logs the request (depending on their data retention settings), and returns a response.

Even with enterprise agreements in place, this means:

- Data passes through and is processed by infrastructure you do not control
- Logging, caching, and abuse-detection systems may retain copies of inputs
- A security incident at the vendor could expose data you sent through their API
- The vendor's privacy policy and data processing addendum govern what happens to your data — not your organization's policies alone

For low-sensitivity work, this is an acceptable tradeoff. For work involving claimant PII or PHI, it is not.

### Local AI Architecture

In a local AI interaction, the flow looks like this:

```
Your device  →  Local model process  →  Response
               (everything in local memory, nothing leaves)
```

Tools like **Ollama** and **LM Studio** download model weights to your local machine and run inference entirely in memory on your CPU or GPU. No network call is made during inference. No data leaves your machine. There is nothing to intercept, log, or expose to a third party.

This is the **gold standard** for handling sensitive data in AI-assisted workflows.

## Three Data Sensitivity Tiers at Nava

Not all data requires the same level of protection. Nava works with a spectrum of data types, and the appropriate AI architecture depends on which tier you're working in.

### Tier 1 — Public Data
Data that is already publicly available or has no personal identifiers. Examples:
- Published federal regulations and policy documents
- Open datasets from data.gov or state open-data portals
- Aggregated, de-identified research findings
- Nava marketing content and public blog posts

**Appropriate for:** Cloud AI tools, including free consumer tiers. No special precautions required.

### Tier 2 — Internal / Operational Data
Data that is internal to Nava or a client agency but does not contain individual claimant information. Examples:
- Internal project documentation and meeting notes
- Anonymized UX research summaries
- System architecture documents
- Vendor contracts (without embedded PII)

**Appropriate for:** Enterprise-tier cloud AI with a signed Data Processing Addendum (DPA) in place. Verify the agreement covers your specific use case before sharing. When in doubt, use local.

### Tier 3 — Sensitive Client / Claimant Data
Data that directly identifies individuals or describes their health, financial, or family circumstances. This tier includes all **PII** and **PHI**. Examples:
- Social Security Numbers
- Full names combined with dates of birth and addresses
- Medicaid / CHIP enrollment records
- SNAP case records
- Child welfare case files
- Tax records or income documentation

**Appropriate for:** Local AI only. This data must never be pasted into a cloud AI interface, even an enterprise one, without explicit legal review and client approval. At Nava, the default answer is **local only**.

## Defining PII and PHI

### Personally Identifiable Information (PII)

**PII** is any information that can be used to identify a specific individual, either directly or in combination with other data. The canonical examples under federal guidance:

- Full name (especially when combined with other identifiers)
- Social Security Number (SSN)
- Date of birth
- Home address or mailing address
- Phone number
- Email address
- Driver's license or government ID number
- Biometric identifiers (fingerprints, facial recognition data)
- IP address (in some contexts)

A single data point may not constitute PII in isolation. Name alone is often not PII. But **name + DOB + address** is almost always PII. When in doubt, treat it as PII.

### Protected Health Information (PHI)

**PHI** is a subset of PII defined by HIPAA. It is health-related information linked to a specific individual. PHI includes:

- Diagnoses, medical conditions, and symptoms
- Treatment records and clinical notes
- Prescription history
- Health insurance plan membership and enrollment
- Claim records and Explanation of Benefits documents
- Mental health and substance use disorder records (subject to additional protections under 42 CFR Part 2)

For Nava's Medicaid and public health program work, a significant portion of claimant data is PHI. It must be handled with particular care.

## Data Sovereignty

**Data sovereignty** is the principle that data is subject to the laws and governance of the jurisdiction or entity that created or owns it.

In the government context, this means:

- Federal agency data is subject to federal law (FISMA, Privacy Act, relevant statutes)
- State agency data may also be subject to state privacy statutes that exceed federal minimums
- Claimant data belongs, in a meaningful sense, to the claimant — and they have expectations about how it is used

When an agency contracts with Nava to build or operate a digital service, they are entrusting us with stewardship of that data. We do not have broad license to process it in new ways just because a technology becomes available. Each new AI use case involving Tier 3 data should be evaluated for:

1. Is there a legal authority for this processing?
2. Has the client agency approved this use?
3. Is the architecture defensible under applicable law?

Local inference is the architecture most easily defensible under data sovereignty principles because the data never leaves the authorized environment.

## Compliance Frameworks

Understanding the major frameworks helps you interpret client requirements and recognize when to escalate.

**FISMA (Federal Information Security Management Act)** — Establishes minimum security standards for federal information systems. Any system that processes federal data must be authorized under FISMA. Relevant to Nava whenever we build or operate on federal infrastructure.

**FedRAMP (Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program)** — A standardized authorization process for cloud services used by federal agencies. Cloud AI APIs must typically be FedRAMP-authorized at the appropriate impact level (Low, Moderate, or High) to be used with federal data. Most commercial AI APIs are not yet authorized at Moderate or High impact.

**HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act)** — Governs PHI handled by covered entities and their **business associates**. Nava frequently operates as a business associate on healthcare programs, which means HIPAA's technical, physical, and administrative safeguard requirements apply directly to us.

**State Privacy Statutes** — California (CCPA/CPRA), Virginia (VCDPA), and others have enacted privacy laws that may impose requirements beyond federal minimums. Some states have standalone health data privacy laws. Always check state-specific requirements with your project's compliance lead.

> **Practical rule:** Default to the most restrictive reasonable interpretation until you have guidance. It is much easier to loosen a restriction than to remediate a privacy incident.

## The Risk Model: Why "Enterprise" Is Not Enough

Enterprise agreements typically include Data Processing Addenda (DPAs), promises not to train on your data, retention limits, and security certifications (SOC 2, ISO 27001). These are meaningful protections — but not zero-risk.

Residual risks remain: a vendor breach can expose data regardless of contractual promises; vendors use subprocessors whose obligations may not be equally rigorous; and specific client contracts may prohibit processing data outside a defined environment even when Nava has an adequate DPA in place.

The enterprise agreement is a floor, not a ceiling. For Tier 3 data, the ceiling is local inference.

## LM Studio and Ollama: Local Inference in Practice

Two tools dominate local AI inference for practitioners:

**Ollama** — A command-line tool and local API server. Pull a model with `ollama pull llama3.1:8b`, run it with `ollama run llama3.1:8b`, and it automatically exposes an OpenAI-compatible API on `localhost:11434`. No data leaves the machine at any point.

**LM Studio** — A graphical desktop application for downloading, managing, and running local models. Better for practitioners who prefer a UI over a terminal. Also exposes a local OpenAI-compatible API endpoint.

Both tools run models entirely on your machine and support the same open-weight model ecosystem (Llama, Mistral, Phi, Qwen, and others). Phase 3 of this course covers full installation and hardware configuration.

## "Bring the Model to the Data" vs. "Bring the Data to the Model"

This framing captures the essential architectural choice:

| Approach | How it works | Privacy profile |
|---|---|---|
| **Bring data to the model** | You send data to a cloud API | Data leaves your control |
| **Bring model to the data** | Model runs where data lives | Data never moves |

For sensitive govtech work, "bring the model to the data" is the Nava standard. This can mean running a model on a practitioner's laptop (personal scale), on an agency-controlled server (program scale), or on an air-gapped system (high-security scale). The principle is the same at every scale.

> **Takeaway:** Your AI architecture is a privacy architecture. Choosing a model is also choosing a data boundary. Make that choice deliberately, document it, and be able to explain it to your client.
