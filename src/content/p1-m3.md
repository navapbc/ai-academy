# AI Ethics in GovTech

Ethics in consumer AI is mostly about user experience and business reputation. Ethics in **civic AI** is about something more consequential: whether people receive housing, healthcare, food assistance, or have their children removed from their home. The stakes are categorically different — and so are the obligations.

> **Core principle:** An AI system deployed in government service delivery is not a neutral tool. It encodes choices about who gets help, how quickly, and on what terms. Those choices require human accountability.

---

## Why Civic AI Ethics Is Different

When a consumer app's recommendation algorithm makes a bad call, you get an irrelevant product suggestion. When a government eligibility system makes a bad call:

- A family loses Medicaid coverage mid-treatment
- A SNAP application is incorrectly denied, and the applicant doesn't know why or how to appeal
- A child welfare screening flags a low-income family as high-risk at higher rates than a wealthier family exhibiting similar behaviors
- A veteran's benefits claim is delayed because an automated classifier misread a handwritten form

The people most affected by these failures are typically those with the least capacity to navigate an appeals process, hire an attorney, or persist through bureaucratic friction. That asymmetry is the defining ethical fact of govtech AI.

Nava exists to reduce that friction — not add a new algorithmic layer to it.

## Algorithmic Bias

**Algorithmic bias** occurs when an AI system produces systematically different outcomes for different demographic groups, often in ways that disadvantage already-marginalized populations.

The mechanism is structural: models learn patterns from historical data. If historical data reflects discriminatory practices — redlining in housing, disparate policing, unequal access to healthcare — the model learns those patterns and perpetuates them in its outputs.

### Where Bias Enters the Pipeline

1. **Training data:** If labeled examples over-represent one group or encode historical inequity, the model learns that inequity as signal.
2. **Feature selection:** Variables that correlate with protected characteristics (zip code as a proxy for race; education level as a proxy for income) can introduce proxy discrimination.
3. **Objective function:** Optimizing for the wrong metric (e.g., minimizing false negatives at the expense of false positives) can create disparate outcomes across groups.
4. **Feedback loops:** Systems that influence future data (a screening tool that shapes which cases get escalated, which in turn shapes future training data) can amplify bias over time.

### A GovTech Example

A predictive model used to prioritize child welfare investigations was trained on historical caseload data. Because low-income families were historically investigated at higher rates (for reasons including mandatory reporter reporting patterns and resource constraints), the model learned income-correlated features as risk signals. It then flagged low-income families at elevated rates — not because they posed greater actual risk, but because they resembled families that had been flagged in the past. The bias was in the history, not the code.

## Disparate Impact

**Disparate impact** is a legal and ethical concept describing when a policy or system that appears neutral on its face produces unequal outcomes across protected groups (race, sex, national origin, disability status, etc.).

A facial-recognition system that performs equally well regardless of race has equal treatment. A system that performs significantly worse for darker-skinned faces has disparate impact — even if the code itself contains no explicit race-based logic.

For Nava programs, disparate impact analysis means asking:

- Does this tool's error rate vary across demographic groups?
- Who bears the cost of false positives vs. false negatives?
- Are the populations most harmed by errors the ones with the least recourse?

A system can have 95% accuracy overall and still be deeply unfair if that remaining 5% error lands almost entirely on one population. Aggregate accuracy metrics hide this. **Disaggregated fairness metrics** surface it.

## Automation Bias

**Automation bias** is the well-documented human tendency to over-trust automated system outputs — to defer to what the screen says even when it conflicts with human judgment or available evidence.

In high-volume casework environments, automation bias is especially dangerous. A worker reviewing 50 cases a day, with a system that flags 10 as high-risk, faces pressure to trust those flags. Investigating all 50 thoroughly would take more time than is available. The system becomes a de facto decision-maker, even if the legal and procedural fiction maintains that a human made the call.

Research on automation bias shows:

- Workers are less likely to override automated recommendations when the system presents its output with high confidence scores, even when those scores are not well-calibrated
- Errors become harder to catch because workers are not independently generating the alternatives the system might be displacing
- Over time, human expertise can atrophy in domains where automation handles the judgment calls

The "it said so on the screen" effect is not a hypothetical. It has been documented in radiological screening, criminal risk assessment, and social services intake. It is a foreseeable outcome of deploying AI in any high-volume professional workflow.

Nava's standard: design for **human agency**, not human rubber-stamping. AI presents options; humans make decisions with full ability to override.

## Explainability vs. Transparency

These terms are often used interchangeably but mean different things in practice.

**Transparency** is about the system: Can you see the model architecture, the training data, the feature weights? Transparency is primarily useful to auditors, researchers, and regulators.

**Explainability** is about the decision: Can you tell *this applicant* *why* their application was flagged, scored, or denied? Explainability is what due process requires.

A system can be technically transparent (open weights, published training methodology) while still being unexplainable to a layperson. And a system can provide plain-language explanations while still running opaque logic under the hood.

For government programs, **explainability is the more critical requirement**. A claimant has a legal right to understand why they were denied and how to appeal. An AI system whose decision logic cannot be translated into human-readable terms is a due process liability — regardless of how accurate it is.

### What Explainability Looks Like in Practice

Not this:
> "Your application was denied based on an automated risk assessment."

This:
> "Your application was flagged because the income reported on your submission ($2,400/month) exceeds the eligibility threshold for this program ($2,200/month). If your income has changed, you may submit updated documentation and request a manual review."

The second version is explainable. A person can read it, verify it, and take action. That's the standard.

## Due Process Implications

**Due process** in the constitutional sense requires that government actions affecting individual rights (to benefits, custody, housing) follow fair procedures — notice, a meaningful opportunity to be heard, and a reasoned decision.

AI systems that make or influence eligibility determinations must be designed with due process in mind:

- **Auditability:** There must be a record of what the system processed and what output it produced, so that a decision can be reconstructed and reviewed
- **Contestability:** Affected individuals must have a meaningful path to challenge automated outputs
- **Human review:** Fully automated adverse decisions in high-stakes contexts are legally and ethically suspect — there should be a human who owns the final call and can explain it

At Nava, this translates directly to design requirements: every AI-influenced workflow in a government program should have an explicit human review step, a logged decision trail, and a clearly documented appeals path.

## Equitable Access

AI tools themselves can create new inequities if they are not designed with accessibility in mind.

- **Connectivity:** A tool that requires high-speed broadband to function excludes rural communities and low-income households that rely on mobile data or spotty connections
- **Hardware:** Local AI tools that require modern multi-core processors or dedicated GPUs exclude practitioners working on older agency-issued hardware
- **Language:** A model fine-tuned primarily on English text may perform significantly worse for Spanish, Haitian Creole, or Somali speakers — populations that are often overrepresented in public benefit programs
- **Literacy:** AI-generated explanations that assume high reading literacy may be inaccessible to a significant portion of the population being served

This isn't abstract. The populations most likely to interact with government benefit AI — Medicaid applicants, SNAP recipients, unhoused individuals navigating shelter systems — are precisely the populations most likely to have connectivity, hardware, and language limitations.

**Equity in AI design means accounting for the full range of conditions under which real users will actually use the system.**

## The Human-in-the-Loop Requirement

**Human-in-the-loop (HITL)** is the principle that consequential automated outputs must be reviewed and approved by a human before they take effect.

At Nava, this is not negotiable for high-stakes decisions. The model:

- **Drafts** communications, flags cases, scores applications, extracts data
- **Does not finalize** benefit determinations, denials, case escalations, or actions that directly affect a person's rights or access to services

This isn't inefficiency — it's the appropriate architecture for a high-stakes domain. AI can dramatically increase the speed and consistency of case processing while humans remain accountable for outcomes. The goal is to give caseworkers better information faster, not to replace their judgment.

> A caseworker who reviews 40 AI-assisted cases per day with genuine judgment is better than one who rubber-stamps 120 AI outputs per day. Volume without accountability is not improvement.

## The Nava Standard: Fairness Metrics, Not Just Accuracy Metrics

Accuracy alone is an insufficient measure of an AI system's performance in civic contexts. Nava's standard requires that any AI system deployed in a program context be evaluated across multiple dimensions:

| Metric type | What it measures | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Accuracy** | Overall correctness | Baseline performance |
| **False positive rate by group** | Error rate on non-cases across demographics | Prevents over-flagging protected groups |
| **False negative rate by group** | Missed cases across demographics | Prevents under-serving protected groups |
| **Calibration** | Whether confidence scores reflect actual accuracy | Affects human override behavior |
| **Explainability score** | Whether outputs can be translated for end users | Due process compliance |
| **Access audit** | Whether the tool functions for low-connectivity / low-literacy users | Equity requirement |

These are evaluated before deployment and on an ongoing basis. Fairness is not a launch-day checklist; it is a continuous measurement practice.

## Responsible AI Is a Practice, Not a Checkbox

Every ethical framework, every fairness metric, every explainability requirement can be satisfied on paper and still fail in practice if the culture around AI deployment isn't one of active, ongoing interrogation.

That means:

- Asking "who is harmed if this is wrong?" before deploying, not after
- Treating model outputs as hypotheses to be verified, not facts to be acted on
- Investing in feedback mechanisms that surface errors from the people most likely to experience them — the end users, not just the internal QA team
- Being willing to pause or roll back AI capabilities when harm is observed, even when it creates operational friction
- Documenting decisions and their rationale so that future teams can evaluate whether those decisions were right

The Nava mission — simple, effective, equitable — is a measuring stick. An AI tool that makes casework faster but less fair is not a win. An AI tool that works beautifully in English but poorly in Spanish is not equitable. An AI tool that a caseworker can't override is not appropriate for government service delivery.

> **Takeaway:** Responsible AI is not the absence of AI. It is AI deployed with eyes open — with clear understanding of its limitations, continuous measurement of its fairness, and humans who remain genuinely accountable for every decision it influences.

## Nava Research: What Caseworkers Actually Experience

The principles above are grounded in documented Nava research — not theory. Nava Labs, in partnership with Georgetown and Cornell universities, conducted a rigorous evaluation of an AI assistive chatbot deployed with benefit navigators and caseworkers across Los Angeles County.

### The Study Design

Two complementary methods:

**Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT):** 125 caseworkers were tested on accuracy using AI-generated responses to hypothetical questions derived from real caseworker experiences.

**Real-world pilot:** A 14-week deployment with 61 caseworkers across six Los Angeles County organizations, using Amplifi's Benefit Navigator tool. Measured via product logs, surveys, and in-depth interviews.

### What the Numbers Show

**Accuracy:** Workers using the AI chatbot showed a **40% average improvement** in accuracy on complex benefits questions — with larger gains on the harder questions. The chatbot used RAG from pre-vetted policy sources only; when a question fell outside its scope, it responded "I don't know the answer" rather than guessing. The system design itself reflects the transparency principle.

**Adoption:** **65% of caseworkers with access used the tool**, with an average of 14 prompts submitted per user. Adoption was highest in rapid-intake settings. Usage declined over time across sites — a signal that ongoing training and organizational support are necessary for sustained adoption, not a product failure.

**User Satisfaction:** Net Promoter Score of **11** — 40% of users were "promoters" who would recommend it to colleagues. Workers valued the tool but adoption required active investment.

**Administrative Burden:** Results on reduced learning and psychological costs were promising but not statistically significant, due to small sample size and lower survey response rates in that portion of the study.

### The Reading Level Gap

The chatbot's source policy manuals were written at a **college reading level**. The AI's responses measured at **10th–12th grade** — meaningfully more accessible than the raw manuals, but still above the federal plain language target of 8th grade for public-facing content.

This is a direct illustration of the equity principle: accuracy and accessibility are independent metrics. A system can retrieve and summarize policy accurately while still producing output that a significant portion of the served population cannot readily parse. Closing this gap requires deliberate prompt engineering — "write at a 6th grade reading level, use short sentences, avoid jargon" — not just accurate retrieval.

Nava's [Readable AI Content Toolkit](https://www.navapbc.com/toolkits/readable-ai-content) provides practical guidance on building readability requirements into AI-assisted content workflows.

### Organizational Context Is an Ethics Variable

The pilot found that "super users" emerged specifically at sites with three conditions: active training participation, organizational reinforcement from managers, and peer support from colleagues who had already integrated the tool.

The same tool, deployed identically in terms of software, produced different outcomes depending on whether the surrounding organization treated it as a capability to develop or a product to install. This is an ethics finding, not just an adoption finding.

A tool rapidly adopted in a low-support environment may produce **automation bias** — over-trust in AI output — before workers have built realistic expectations of where the tool is right and where it fails. Implementation design, training, and human review structures are not afterthoughts to responsible AI deployment. They are part of it.
