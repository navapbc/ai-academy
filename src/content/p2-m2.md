# The Nava Use-Case Library

Not every AI use case is created equal. In government service delivery, the consequences of a wrong output can affect someone's access to housing, healthcare, or food assistance. Nava's approach is to identify the **sweet spot**: use cases where AI delivers measurable productivity or quality gains, while the risk of a harmful mistake is low or fully mitigated by human review.

> **The sweet spot principle:** High-impact tasks where AI drafts and a human expert reviews. Avoid use cases where AI outputs become final decisions without meaningful human oversight.

## The Risk Scoring Framework

Before deploying any AI-assisted workflow, Nava evaluates use cases on two dimensions:

| Dimension | What to ask |
|---|---|
| **Impact** | How much time or quality does this save? Does it serve residents directly? |
| **Likelihood of harm** | What happens if the output is wrong? Who is affected? |

Multiply impact × (1 / harm likelihood) to get a relative priority score. High impact + low harm potential = deploy with cloud AI. High impact + high harm potential = deploy locally only, or defer until safeguards exist.

## Four Approved Use-Case Categories

### 1. Document Summarization

Summarizing internal memos, research reports, meeting notes, or public policy documents. The human always reads the summary alongside the source — the AI is saving reading time, not replacing judgment.

**Approved scope:** Public documents, internal Nava documents with no PII, de-identified research materials.

**Example:** A Nava researcher pastes in a 40-page HHS report and asks the model to extract the three most significant findings for a state Medicaid agency. The researcher verifies against the source before including the summary in a deliverable.

### 2. Plain Language Rewriting

Federal and state agencies are legally required to communicate in plain language under the **PLAIN Act (Plain Writing Act of 2010)**. In practice, benefit notices and eligibility letters routinely fail this standard, using jargon and passive constructions that confuse applicants.

AI can rewrite dense regulatory language to an **8th-grade reading level** — the federal baseline for public-facing government communications — in seconds.

**Key terms:**
- **Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level:** A readability score estimating the U.S. school grade needed to comprehend a text. Target ≤ 8 for public benefits communications.
- **PLAIN Act:** Requires federal agencies to use clear, concise language in new or substantially revised public-facing documents.

**Example prompt:**
```
Rewrite the following eligibility notice at an 8th-grade reading level.
Use short sentences, active voice, and plain vocabulary.
Do not change any factual content — only the language.

[Original notice text]
```

**Human review step:** A policy SME confirms no factual content was altered before the revised language is shared with clients or agency partners.

### 3. Policy Q&A with Grounding

Using the RAG architecture (covered in Module 1) to answer caseworker or researcher questions against a curated library of policy documents. The model cites the specific section it used and flags when it cannot find a relevant answer.

**Example:** A caseworker asks "Does this client's SSI income count toward the SNAP gross income test?" The system retrieves the relevant SNAP policy manual section and provides an answer with the citation. The caseworker reviews the cited source before acting.

**Critical constraint:** The AI explains what the policy says. It does not make a determination about an individual's case. That determination remains with the human caseworker.

### 4. Synthetic Persona Testing

Creating realistic simulated user profiles to test form flows, notice language, or digital services — without using real claimant data.

**The technique:** Prompt the model with a detailed persona description, then ask it to interact with a prototype or evaluate a document from that persona's perspective.

**Example persona prompt:**
```
Act as a 65-year-old rural resident with limited English proficiency who is
applying for Medicaid for the first time. You have a 6th-grade reading level,
no smartphone, and access only to a shared computer at the local library.
Review the following enrollment form and describe every point of confusion
you encounter.
```

This technique generates accessibility and plain language feedback in minutes rather than weeks of user research scheduling. It does not replace actual user research — it **augments** it by identifying obvious failure points before recruiting real participants.

## Out-of-Scope Uses

Some use cases are explicitly not approved at Nava, regardless of how capable the underlying model is:

- **AI making eligibility determinations:** Whether a person qualifies for a benefit is a legal and ethical decision that must remain with a trained human caseworker or adjudicator.
- **AI replacing human caseworkers:** AI can reduce administrative burden, but client-facing communication and case decisions require human accountability.
- **AI in legal or appeals decisions:** Any process where an AI output could directly affect a legal right requires human review and proper due process protections.
- **Using cloud AI with real PII:** Claimant names, SSNs, case numbers, and medical information must stay local. See Phase 1 privacy architecture modules.

## Administrative Burden Reduction

One of the highest-ROI applications of AI in govtech is identifying where processes break down — without making any consequential decisions. AI can:

- Flag form fields that consistently generate errors or abandonment (by analyzing anonymized completion data descriptions)
- Identify policy language that generates the highest volume of caseworker clarification calls
- Surface redundant data collection steps where the same information is asked multiple times across a benefit application

These are **analytical** tasks. The AI surfaces patterns; Nava staff and agency partners decide what to do about them.

## The "AI as Co-Pilot" Principle

Every AI-assisted workflow at Nava follows this structure:

```
1. Human expert defines the task and provides context
2. AI produces a draft output
3. Human expert reviews, edits, and approves
4. Human expert's name is on the final deliverable
```

The model is a force multiplier, not an autonomous agent. This principle protects clients, maintains accountability, and ensures Nava staff develop — rather than atrophy — their own subject matter expertise.

## Documenting AI-Assisted Work

When AI tools are used to produce client deliverables, Nava follows an internal disclosure standard:

- Note in the document footer or metadata that AI-assisted drafting was used
- Identify which sections were AI-drafted vs. human-authored
- Retain the original prompts and model outputs for at least 90 days
- If a client asks whether AI was used, answer honestly

This is not just an ethics requirement — it is a quality control practice. Retaining prompts and outputs lets you audit the work if questions arise later.

## Key Takeaways

1. Use the impact × harm framework to decide whether a use case is appropriate and whether it requires local or cloud AI.
2. The four approved categories (summarization, plain language, policy Q&A, synthetic personas) cover most day-to-day Nava needs.
3. AI never makes eligibility, legal, or case decisions — those stay with humans.
4. Every AI-assisted deliverable carries a human reviewer's accountability.
5. Document your AI use — prompt, output, and review — for auditability.

## Nava Labs: The AI Appropriateness Framework

Before building or deploying any AI tool, Nava follows a four-phase evaluation framework developed through its public sector work:

### Phase 1 — Define the Problem
Start with deep research with end users, beneficiaries, and frontline staff. What specific task is slow, error-prone, or inaccessible? What does a measurable improvement look like? **Do not select a technology before defining the problem.**

### Phase 2 — Determine AI Appropriateness
Not every problem is an AI problem.

- **Traditional AI is strong at:** pattern recognition, prediction, classification, recommendation
- **Generative AI is strong at:** information retrieval, summarization, translation, content generation
- **AI is poorly suited for:** tasks requiring ethical judgment, tasks requiring social trust, tasks where errors have irreversible consequences without human review

Always compare to the non-AI alternative. If a well-designed form or a clearer policy document solves the problem, that is the right answer.

### Phase 3 — Assess Readiness
- Is the underlying data high-quality, representative, and current?
- Is there a privacy compliance pathway (HIPAA, state statute, contract terms)?
- Does the team have the technical infrastructure and staff capacity to maintain the tool?
- Is there a testing and QA process that includes the people most likely to be harmed by errors?

### Phase 4 — Evaluate Risks
- What are the known failure modes and their downstream effects?
- Does the system perform equitably across demographic groups?
- What are the human review and fallback options if the system produces a wrong answer?
- How will the system be monitored after launch?

> **The core principle:** Benefits must demonstrably outweigh risks — and "benefits" must be measured in outcomes for real users, not impressiveness in a demo.

### Evidence from the LA Caseworker Study

Nava's randomized study of the benefits chatbot across Los Angeles County provides a clear-eyed view of what real-world AI deployment looks like:

- **40% accuracy improvement** on complex benefits questions (the win)
- Reading level of AI responses: **10th–12th grade** — better than policy manuals, but still above the 8th-grade federal target (the gap to close)
- Net Promoter Score of **11** — caseworkers valued it but adoption wasn't automatic
- Adoption was highest at organizations that provided **structured training and peer support** — "super users" emerged where engagement was cultivated

**The lesson:** A technically sound AI tool can still fail in practice if implementation support is weak. Technology is one layer; organizational change management is another.
