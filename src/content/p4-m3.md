## The Nava AI Maturity Ladder

Where are you now? Where are you headed?

**Level 1 — Consumer**
Using AI tools ad hoc: pasting things into ChatGPT, running occasional Copilot suggestions, using tools built by others. No systematic thinking about data safety or output quality.

**Level 2 — Practitioner**
Grounded prompts, local models for sensitive data, deliberate evaluation of AI output. You know when to trust the model and when to verify. This is the minimum bar for Nava project work involving client data.

**Level 3 — Builder**
Creating harnessed AI tools for your project: a team-specific system prompt library, a local RAG knowledge base for a policy domain, a custom AI persona for a client deliverable. You treat prompts like code.

**Level 4 — Architect**
Designing AI systems for teams. Defining standards, setting data boundaries, mentoring others on responsible use, contributing to Nava's internal AI playbook. You think about failure modes before you think about features.

Most people finishing this sprint are between Level 1 and Level 2. The goal is to get to Level 2 reliably — and to have enough context to reach Level 3 when your project needs it.

## The Six Principles You Carry Forward

These are not rules posted in a wiki. They are the operating principles behind every decision you'll make about AI on a Nava project.

**1. Local first for sensitive data — always.**
If data is HIPAA-regulated, covered by a data use agreement, or simply something a client has entrusted to you, it does not go to a cloud AI service without explicit approval. Full stop. Ollama runs on your laptop. Use it.

**2. Ground before you trust — always cite your source.**
An ungrounded model answer is a hypothesis, not a finding. Before using any AI-generated claim in a deliverable, find the primary source and cite it. If you can't find the source, the claim doesn't go in.

**3. Human in the loop — AI drafts, people decide.**
AI output that affects real people's access to services, benefits, or rights is not a final product. It is a first draft. A caseworker, policy analyst, or service designer reviews and decides. Document who reviewed and when.

**4. Measure fairness, not just accuracy.**
A model that is 95% accurate overall but systematically wrong for a particular demographic group is not a success — it is a liability and an equity failure. Always ask: accurate for whom? Disaggregate your evaluations.

**5. Treat prompts like code — version control, test, document.**
Your system prompt is software. Put it in git. Write a changelog. Define test cases. Review changes before deploying to production. The quality of your AI output is a direct function of the quality of your prompt engineering.

**6. Keep learning — the field changes every quarter.**
A capability that doesn't exist today will exist in three months. A tool that seems dominant today may be irrelevant in a year. The specific tools matter less than your ability to evaluate new ones using the mental models from this course.

## The Civic Tech AI Landscape in 2025–2026

The environment you're operating in is shifting rapidly. Here is what to watch.

**Federal AI guidance is evolving.** OMB has issued memos on responsible AI use in federal agencies. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework is becoming the reference standard for federal AI procurement and deployment. If you're working on a federal program, you need to know what RMF tier your AI use falls into.

**Local government AI pilots are proliferating.** State and county agencies are piloting AI for benefits intake, document processing, and constituent services. The quality of these pilots varies enormously. Nava has an opportunity to model what responsible, human-centered AI deployment looks like in this space.

**Open-source models are closing the gap.** The performance difference between the best open-source models and frontier proprietary models has narrowed dramatically. For many govtech tasks — document summarization, classification, structured extraction — a local model is now good enough. This gap will continue to close.

**MCP is standardizing tool integration.** The Model Context Protocol is being adopted across the industry. Tools you build to MCP standards today will be compatible with future model clients. Investing in MCP-based integrations is building on a foundation that won't require rewriting every time a new model is released.

## A Note on Pace

The history of technology is full of tools that felt transformative for two years and then became infrastructure — invisible, assumed, ubiquitous. AI will follow the same pattern. The urgent feeling of "I need to learn everything right now" will ease.

What won't ease is the importance of asking good questions about any AI system: Who does this serve? Who does this exclude? Who reviews the output? Where does the data go? What happens when it's wrong?

Those questions are not AI-specific. They are Nava-specific. They are the questions that have always driven the best work here.

## Recognizing AI Washing

One skill this sprint gives you that is easy to undervalue: the ability to recognize hype. The govtech market will be flooded with vendors claiming AI capabilities that are shallow, biased, or simply rebranded keyword matching. You now have enough grounding to ask the hard questions:

- What model powers this? Is it local, cloud, or a hybrid?
- Where does the data go? Who has data residency agreements with whom?
- How was the model evaluated? On what dataset? By whom?
- What happens when the model is wrong? Who is liable?
- Has this been tested for disparate impact across demographic groups?

These questions are not adversarial. They are due diligence. Any vendor worth working with will have good answers. Any vendor that deflects or says "our AI is proprietary" without further explanation is a risk.
